### The five biggest discoveries so far

If I had to prioritize what could actually make our NFL/QB model substantially better, **M04, M05, M06, M08 and M11** jump out.

**M04 — Early-down success → third-down environment.** This may be one of the missing links in projecting QB outcomes. A quarterback doesn't independently generate “35 attempts for 250 yards.” His distribution is partly determined by how often the offense encounters 3rd-and-7 versus 3rd-and-2.

That suggests a chain such as:

**1st-down outcome → 2nd-down state → 3rd-down probability/distance → defensive pressure package → QB outcome**

rather than directly predicting QB yards from opponent averages.

**M05/M06 — Pressure opportunity and pressure suppression.** This is particularly valuable. Pregame we correctly identified Denver's pass rush versus KC's LT as a mismatch. But KC demonstrated that a matchup can be **real yet strategically dormant**.

We therefore shouldn't simply calculate:

> Denver pressure grade − KC pass-block grade = expected pressure.

We need something closer to:

**Expected Pressure Exposure = Dropbacks × Long-Dropback Rate × Protection Weakness × Opponent Rush Quality × (1 − Quick-Game/RPO Suppression)**

That's much more sophisticated.

**M08 — High-leverage targets.** Bryant is showing why raw target share leaves information on the table.

Imagine two receivers each get eight targets:

Receiver A gets eight targets on 1st-and-10.

Receiver B gets four ordinary targets, two third-down targets and two red-zone targets.

They shouldn't have identical role grades.

I want our receiver/QB system eventually calculating something like:

**Target Leverage Score**

where third down, red zone, two-minute, pressure and fourth-down targets receive additional contextual weight.

**M11 — Snap-level injury state.** This could materially improve historical backtesting.

A QB might finish:

**26/34, 292 yards, 3 TD**

against a supposedly elite secondary.

But if CB1 left after 18 snaps and CB2 left after 34 snaps, feeding the game into the training set as:

> QB shredded elite defense

teaches the model the wrong lesson.

Historical opponent strength needs to become:

**Opponent strength actually faced while the QB was on the field.**

## Another thing Q1 taught us: separate prediction errors from football randomness

The Nix interception is a good example.

If Nix throws one interception, that doesn't immediately prove our Nix projection was poor. Interceptions are highly stochastic.

But if the **mechanism** behind it was something predictable — say Spagnuolo rotated from a two-high presentation into a robber look and Nix repeatedly fails to recognize it — *that* is predictive.

So every live observation should now get two tags:

**Outcome:** what happened.

**Mechanism:** why it happened.

We should update our model much more aggressively from a repeated **mechanism** than from one outcome.

That's arguably **M26** for the checklist:

**M26 — Outcome vs mechanism classification.**
Model updates should occur primarily when new information changes the underlying causal mechanism, not merely because a TD/INT happened.

I'd mark that **⭐ Critical**.

**M27 — Penalty-created game states ⭐ Critical**

Penalties should not just appear as “penalty yards.”

They alter the entire decision tree.

A holding call that turns something like 2nd-and-manageable into **2nd-and-19** changes:

* expected pass probability,
* expected ADOT,
* blitz probability,
* sack probability,
* checkdown probability,
* conversion probability,
* eventual punt probability.

For QB modeling, that's especially important. If Nix subsequently throws two short completions and Denver punts, a box-score model could treat that as poor QB efficiency.

But the causal sequence was:

**successful offensive play → penalty → long-yardage state → constrained passing → punt.**

So I'd add a variable such as:

**Drive Disruption State**

with flags for:

* offensive holding,
* false start,
* sack,
* negative rush,
* offensive pass interference,
* pre-snap penalty.

Then estimate how those disruptions change the remainder of the drive.

**M44 — Play-call quality must be separated from play result ⭐⭐⭐⭐ CORE**

This is crucial.

Suppose a coach calls an excellent concept on 3rd-and-1 and a defender makes an unbelievable individual play.

**Good call, bad result.**

Conversely, suppose the coach calls a terrible concept and Walker breaks two tackles for the first down.

**Bad call, good result.**

If we train from outcomes alone, the model learns the wrong lesson.

Ultimately we should estimate:

> **Expected success probability of chosen play − expected success probability of available alternatives**

That gives us something resembling **Play-Call Value Added**.

For example:

3rd-and-1
KC defense showing light box
Denver chooses outside run

Expected conversion:

* QB sneak: 82%
* inside run: 74%
* quick pass: 67%
* chosen outside run: 58%

Even if the outside run happens to convert, the caller may have made a **negative-EV decision**.

That's very similar to how we're thinking about betting itself.

That could be valuable for live rushing props as well.

---

## There's also an important data-cleaning lesson

Suppose tomorrow someone examines KC's fourth-quarter running backs without context.

They'll see Johnson receiving a cluster of carries and may conclude:

> Johnson has an expanded offensive role.

That's probably wrong.

His role expanded because the **game entered terminal closeout state**.

So M80 — garbage/closeout contamination — gets another major confirmation.

When estimating normal RB usage, these snaps should either be excluded or labeled separately.

Otherwise our next-game projection could incorrectly increase Johnson's expected workload.

## State → Decision → Mechanism → Outcome

**State:** +21, 4:32 remaining, already in scoring territory.

**Decision:** remove high-value starter exposure and give RB2 consecutive carries.

**Mechanism:** low-variance runs keep the clock moving while protecting Mahomes/Walker.

**Outcome:** reach the two-minute warning with virtually no meaningful comeback path remaining.

Even if this possession produces **zero points**, it is an extremely successful possession strategically.

That's now the second strong validation tonight that **drive success cannot always equal points scored**.
